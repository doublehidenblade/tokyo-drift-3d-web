**Answer (model summary of the grounded search).**

1. **Emergence of *Gado-shita* (1945–1960s, Yurakucho/Shimbashi/Ueno):** Following the end of WWII in 1945, black markets (*yami-ichi*) sprang up beneath the elevated rail tracks (*gādo-shita*) around Ueno (Ameyoko), Yurakucho, and Shimbashi; these quickly evolved into permanent micro-workshops, parts recyclers, tool shops, and food stalls.

2. **Viaduct Bay Dimensions (Tokyo Yamanote Viaducts):** The historic red-brick and masonry arch viaducts running between Shimbashi, Yurakucho, and Tokyo Station (built 1910–1914) typically featured arch spans (bay widths) of approximately 4 to 6 meters, with crown ceiling heights around 3.5 to 5 meters.

3. **Viaduct Bay Dimensions (Postwar Concrete Viaducts & Shuto Expressway):** Mid-Showa reinforced concrete viaducts (such as those constructed for the Shuto Expressway from 1962 onward and elevated rail extensions) generally provided rectangular pillar-and-beam bays with clear widths of 5 to 8 meters and vertical clearances between 3.5 and 5.5 meters.

4. **Workshop Facades and Enclosures:** Repair shops and metalworking bays were enclosed with galvanized corrugated sheet steel (*namiban* / *ton-tan*), raw timber framing, and corrugated translucent wire-glass or PVC skylight sections; entrances used heavy, hand-pulled corrugated roller shutters (*shattā*) with recessed floor guides.

5. **Open-Front Layouts:** Most active daytime repair workshops operated with completely open fronts to ventilate exhaust, maximize alleyway light, and allow machinery or vehicles to encroach directly into the narrow access lanes.

6. **Work Lighting and Interior Fittings:** Workspaces were illuminated by bare, incandescent work lamps fitted with green or white enameled conical tin shades, handheld caged "trouble lights" on hanging coiled cords, and later cold-white overhead fluorescent tube fixtures (*keikōtō*).

7. **Signage Typologies:** Exterior signage consisted primarily of hand-painted wooden or sheet-metal boards (*kanban*), often using black, red, or dark blue Japanese characters on oxidized white/off-white backgrounds, along with vertical wooden panel slats beside shutters indicating trades (e.g., *鉄工所* [ironworks], *修理* [repairs], *部品* [parts]).

8. **Spaces Under the Shuto Expressway (Yokohama & Tokyo, 1960s–1970s):** The dark spaces beneath the elevated Shuto expressways (such as routes traversing Ginza/Kyobashi waterways or Yokohama's inner channels) accommodated low-clearance vehicle body shops, freight sorting sheds, and informal municipal depot stalls bounded by chain-link fences and sheet metal.

9. **Materials of Kotobuki-cho Shanty Dwellings (Yokohama, 1950s–1960s):** Before the mid-1960s shift toward concrete flop-house tenements (*doya*), the day-laborer settlement in Kotobuki-cho consisted of makeshift shanties built from scrap timber, discarded wooden cargo pallets, tar paper, flattened oil drums, salvaged corrugated zinc sheets, and blue or black waterproof tarpaulins weighted down with car tires and cinder blocks.

10. **Exact Footprints of Kotobuki-cho Early Barracks:** Unknown (pre-tenement shanty interior dimensions varied haphazardly based on salvage availability, though early shared rooms were informally subdivided down to 2- to 3-tatami mat spaces per occupant).
