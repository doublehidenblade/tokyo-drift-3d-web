**Question.** The Tsukiji Central Wholesale Market (Tokyo, opened 1935, in use through the Showa era): the fan-shaped market hall and its fish wholesaler stalls - structure (steel trusses, roof form, skylights), clear height, bay widths, wall materials, loading frontage, the turret trucks and styrofoam boxes, signage on stalls. Also typical Showa provincial fish market sheds (e.g. Hakodate, Kanazawa Omicho). Give dimensions in metres where known.

**Answer (model summary of the grounded search).**

* **Plan geometry and origin**: Designed by the Tokyo Municipal Government's Architecture Section and completed in 1934 (opened 1935), Tsukiji's main fish market hall followed an iconic quarter-circle (fan-shaped) arc of approximately 90 degrees; the curvature was directly determined by the minimum turning radius required to pull freight trains off the Shiodome rail line onto curved unloading tracks.
* **Truss structure and clear heights**: The hall was constructed with a monumental riveted structural steel skeleton using radial and chord-aligned Pratt/Warren steel roof trusses spanning the open floor; interior clear height to the underside of the trusses was approximately 8 to 10 m (exact structural clear height varied across the radial sweep and is unknown in original municipal records).
* **Bay widths and column grid**: Structural bay widths along the radial frame generally ranged between 6.0 m and 9.0 m (exact uniform structural column module is unknown due to wedge geometry).
* **Roof form and skylights**: The expansive low-slope gabled roof featured raised longitudinal monitor roofs (clerestories/lanterns) fitted with wired glass skylight glazing and continuous side louvers, maximizing natural indirect daylighting while venting condensation and heat without exposing fish to direct sunlight.
* **Wall and floor materials**: Perimeter walls comprised a hybrid of reinforced concrete plinths and corrugated cement-asbestos or corrugated galvanized steel sheeting on steel girts, punctuated by massive open roll-up or sliding bays; floorings were heavy unsealed concrete with central drainage gutters graded for continuous saltwater washdowns.
* **Loading frontage and logistics platforms**: The outer convex curve functioned as a continuous covered railway platform/truck loading apron, while the inner concave yard provided an expansive truck maneuvering apron; the original freight platform edge had a standard dock height of approximately 1.0 to 1.1 m above rail/ground level.
* **Intermediate wholesaler stalls (*nakaoroshi*)**: The fan interior housed over 700–800 individual wholesaler booths divided along narrow aisles (aisle widths typically 1.5 to 2.0 m); each individual stall module was compact—roughly 1.8 to 2.2 m wide by 1.8 to 2.5 m deep (about 1 *tsubo*, ~3.3 m²)—featuring a wet cutting deck, wooden cutting blocks (*manaita*), and overhead water hoses.
* **Stall signage**: Suspended wooden or painted sheet-metal signs hung from horizontal angle-iron racks at a clearance height of roughly 2.0 to 2.2 m above the aisles, featuring black-on-white or white-on-blue hand-painted trade names (*yagō*), shop license numbers, and crest marks.
* **Turret trucks (*tareto*)**: Developed during the Showa era, three-wheeled motorized cylindrical battery/gasoline turret trucks (manufactured primarily by Asahi Transport and Fuji Heavy Industries)—measuring roughly 2.8 to 3.2 m in overall length, 1.0 to 1.2 m in width, and turning 360° on their front drive wheel—navigated the tight radial aisles pulling flat trailers.
* **Packaging transition (wood to EPS)**: While early-to-mid Showa relied on wooden flat boxes (*ki-bako*), the late Showa era (1970s–1980s) saw total dominance of standardized expanded polystyrene (EPS / styrofoam) fish boxes, typically stacked 1.8 to 2.2 m high directly along stall frontages and walkways.
* **Provincial fish markets (Hakodate / Kanazawa Omicho)**: Provincial Showa-era shed structures (such as Hakodate's coastal market terminals or the rebuilt post-war arcades of Kanazawa's Omicho Market) differed from Tsukiji’s massive radial steel frame, instead employing simple linear single- or multi-span steel portal frames or low-cost timber post-and-beam trusses.
* **Provincial shed dimensions and materiality**: Typical regional market sheds featured clear ceiling heights of 3.5 to 5.5 m, bay spacing of 4.5 to 6.0 m, translucent corrugated PVC/polycarbonate roof daylighting panels, open-air roll-shutter frontages (aisle widths ~2.0 to 3.0 m), and shallow tiled display counters with ice troughs rather than mechanized rail/turret docks.
